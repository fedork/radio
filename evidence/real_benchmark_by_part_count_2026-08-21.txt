Does the algorithm need to differ by state length? Tested across 3, 4, and 8 parts, 2026-08-21
Status: measured. Continues real_benchmark_residual_control_2026-08-21.txt with the same protocol
(real oracle calls, no offline proxy) applied to real documented states of different part counts,
prompted directly by a user hypothesis: "algorithms is likely to be different depending on state
length... 1-2-4-segment states all have different optimal algorithms... 8 is probably different
from 4."

States chosen from the documented record (not cherry-picked for this test): the 4-part residual
positive control already used; a 3-part exact positive with only 2 winning splits of 37.7M total
(`Sb(110:3,115:2,121:1)@7`, docs/results.md); and the 4-part-generic "hard eight-part positive"
`Sb(15:3,14:3,17:2,8:4,11:2,10:2,19:1,15:1)@5` (docs/journal.md), historically 12,585 CPU seconds /
18,857,614 evaluations under default order, still timing out at a 300s cap against a warmed cache
in one attempt. 1- and 2-part states are omitted: this repo already solves those exactly (singleton
majorization; the exhaustive two-part pair table) with no search at all, so there is no ordering
question to test there.

=====================================================================================
RESULTS
=====================================================================================
  parts  state                                            raw space      R_0 survivors   learned-order candidates to success
  3      Sb(110:3,115:2,121:1)@7                           37,700,928 *   204 (exact)      43
  4      Sb(29:6,19:9,13:12,36:3)@6                          ~4.4e6 (stage-2 exact)   809,706 (exact)   67  (documented default: 37,899)
  8      Sb(15:3,14:3,17:2,8:4,11:2,10:2,19:1,15:1)@5        1.4e13 *      2,577 (of a 15M-candidate beam sample)   52

  * raw space = full (n_i+1)(m_i+1) product, before any filtering.

All three witnesses independently re-verified in a fresh process, separate from the run that found
them: mass arithmetic exact (children's masses sum to the parent's, in every case), all three
children genuinely `SOLVABLE` per a second oracle call.

**The candidates-to-success number (43, 67, 52) is remarkably stable across a 3-to-8 part range and
nine orders of magnitude of raw search space.** That is the opposite of what "different part counts
need different algorithms" would predict for the SCORING/ORDERING step specifically: the same value
model (trained once, on 4-part synthetic states, never retrained per part count) and the same
min(V(child)) composition rule ordered a workable witness into the top ~50-70 at 3, 4, AND 8 parts.

=====================================================================================
WHAT ACTUALLY HAD TO CHANGE BY PART COUNT: GENERATING THE CANDIDATES, NOT SCORING THEM
=====================================================================================
The hypothesis is confirmed, but not where expected. The exact-DP candidate enumeration
(`exact_topk.py`'s method, used cleanly for the 3- and 4-part states above) broke outright at 8
parts:

  attempt 1, exact DP, no bound:                    OOM-killed (RSS climbing past several GB,
                                                       process silently vanished)
  attempt 2, pure random sampling (2M draws,
    raw per-part range):                             only 3 R_0 survivors -- too sparse to test
  attempt 3, random sampling from each part's own
    per-part-Pareto-prefiltered options (2M draws):  only 4 R_0 survivors -- still too sparse
  attempt 4, width-bounded incremental DP,
    beam=3,000,000:                                  513 R_0 survivors, but NONE was a winner
  attempt 5, same, beam=15,000,000:                  2,577 R_0 survivors, learned order finds one
                                                       at candidate #52

Four rewrites of the *generator* were needed to get a usable candidate pool at 8 parts; zero
changes were needed to the *scorer*. The width-bounded incremental DP (build the (S,X) combination
one part at a time like the exact method, but randomly subsample down to a fixed beam width
whenever an intermediate step would exceed it, rather than letting it grow unboundedly) is a cheap
approximation of exactly the "DP top-k decoder" the design note already called for as the
deployable architecture -- this session needed it just to MEASURE, which is itself evidence it will
be needed to DEPLOY at high part counts.

Intermediate step sizes for the 8-part state make the scale concrete: even after cap-filtering,
raw survivor counts at intermediate steps reached 59M, 194M, 131M, and 174M before each was capped
back down to the beam width. The filtered candidate space is not "large," it is large enough that
naive generation strategies fail in qualitatively different ways depending on which one you try.

=====================================================================================
WHAT THIS DOES AND DOES NOT SHOW
=====================================================================================
* n=1 per part count. Three real states, chosen from the documented record, not a systematic
  sweep. A single 8-part success does not establish that this generalizes to the near-saturated,
  often-UNSOLVABLE 8-part states that dominate k=6 cost (see below) -- this one is a genuine
  positive, chosen because it already has a documented difficulty figure to compare against.
* The 8-part comparison is against a historical default-order figure (18,857,614 evaluations)
  from a different build/cache era, not a fresh same-build measurement -- unlike the 4-part
  control, where the 37,899 figure was independently confirmed identical across two different
  engine builds. Treat 52-vs-18.86M as indicative of scale, not as a precise ratio.
* **Retracted, 2026-08-21 (same day):** an earlier version of this note read the "0 of 165 8-part
  k=6 states solvable" figure (docs/journal.md, 2026-08-08) as a structural fact and scoped 8-part
  k=6 out of this thread as "a pure refutation regime." That is not established. Those 165 states
  came from one specific solver run's log, not a neutral sample of all 8-part k=6 states, and
  whether that run's selection was itself biased toward negatives (e.g. because it walked a
  refutation-heavy search) has not been checked. Solvable 8-part k=6 states may well exist and be
  under-represented in that particular log for reasons having nothing to do with k=6/8-part being
  intrinsically unsolvable — exactly the kind of corpus-provenance question this repo's own trap
  table already warns about for a different pair of corpora. No claim about 8-part k=6
  solvability, either direction, is made here. The 8-part state actually tested above is at k=5.
* 1- and 2-part states were not tested because there is nothing to test: both are already solved
  exactly, with no split search, by existing non-learned methods in this codebase.
* Not yet wired into radiobase.c, still real-oracle-as-black-box validation.
