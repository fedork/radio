Concentric round expansion (iterative deepening over segment radius), tested against the real
persistent oracle, 2026-08-22
Status: measured. Tool: tools/ml/proto_concentric_rounds.py.

DESIGN. Prompted by a specific critique of the current solver's arbitrariness: `canSolveB_ctx`
walks a state's segments (parts) as nested loops with a shared CPU-unit budget that has no clean
relationship to "how far into each segment's own list have we gotten." The proposal: split a
state's P segments into P-1 "concentric" segments and 1 "last" segment; grow each concentric
segment's own radius R_i(r) geometrically each round; try every new joint combination within the
current radius; and for each, walk the last segment IN FULL, scored with the real, accurate pooled
recursive-V score (cheap here -- only that one segment's own list is free, the coordinate-descent
"single free block" trick). Two design refinements worked out analytically before any code:

  - the per-segment growth factor must be G^(1/(P-1)) for a target TOTAL per-round work growth G
    (not G itself) -- growing every segment by the same factor G makes total work grow by G^(P-1)
    per round, an explosion the conversation flagged before building anything;
  - propagating the ABSOLUTE radius down to child levels (not a relative fraction) automatically
    gives children more RELATIVE effort as state size shrinks, since the same absolute R covers
    an ever-larger fraction of a smaller list -- untested here (single level only, see below), but
    the reasoning is sound: ambiguous (MAYBE) children are actively costly to a parent's own
    search, and children are cheaper to resolve definitively because their spaces are smaller.

Both design points are in the module docstring and the conversation record; this note only tests
the concentric-round mechanism at a single level of the AND-OR tree, not the multi-level
propagation.

=====================================================================================
1. OFFLINE SANITY CHECK BEFORE SPENDING ORACLE CALLS
=====================================================================================
A dry run against known census winners (membership check, no real oracle calls) on U000368 found
both winners reachable at round 16 (radius ~41 in each of 3 outer segments), costing ~7,500-8,000
pooled-score evaluations -- roughly the SAME order as directly scoring the endpoint's full
7,666-candidate R_0-survivor list. This was a sobering signal before running anything real: the
round STRUCTURE alone does not obviously save pooled-scoring work at this scale. The real question
was always whether it saves ORACLE calls (the expensive resource this is meant to reduce), not
Python-side scoring -- which is what the real test below actually measures.

=====================================================================================
2. REAL RESULT: ALL 4 CHARACTERIZED ENDPOINTS SUCCEED
=====================================================================================
Same 4 real k7-census endpoints as evidence/deficit_order_and_bestfirst_2026-08-22.txt (parts
looked up live from CORPORA["k7"]). Outer segments ordered by the deficit signal (U000368 also
tested with blind/natural outer order for comparison -- see section 3).

  endpoint   pooled rank_learned  rank_natural (blind)  coord.descent (section 6, prior note)  concentric round search
  U000368    1                    6,041                 SUCCESS, 18,447 evals                   SUCCESS, round 16, 1,539 oracle calls  [99s]
  U000535    13                   >8,000 (capped)       SUCCESS, 7,046 evals                     SUCCESS, round 18, 4,399 oracle calls  [267s]
  U000607    85                   5,860                 FAILED, 150 restarts, 257,591 evals       SUCCESS, round 17, 3,173 oracle calls  [192s]
  U000068    687                  >8,000 (capped)       FAILED, 150 restarts, 774,712 evals       SUCCESS, round 16, 2,652 oracle calls  [217s]

**All 4 succeed, including both endpoints (U000607, U000068) where block coordinate descent failed
decisively even after 150 restarts and up to 774,712 evaluations.** This is the headline result:
unlike a stochastic local-search method that can get permanently stuck in the wrong basin,
round-based expansion is exhaustive within its current radius by construction -- it cannot get
"stuck" the way restart-based local search can, because every round eventually covers every
combination close enough to the segments' own good orderings, and the radius keeps growing until
it does.

**The oracle-call cost is also far more consistent than either alternative**: 1,539-4,399 across
all 4 endpoints, spanning pooled ranks from 1 to 687 -- a ~3x range in cost across a ~700x range in
how hard the endpoint was for the pooled model. Compare to natural/blind order (5,860 to >8,000,
sometimes not resolving at all) and to coordinate descent (7,046-18,447 where it succeeds at all,
or complete failure costing hundreds of thousands of evaluations where it doesn't). Round-based
search degrades GRACEFULLY with difficulty instead of falling off a cliff -- exactly the
"non-arbitrary, principled stopping point" property the design was proposed for: a round cap
around 20 would very likely resolve this entire tested population, and hitting that cap would be a
meaningful signal (this endpoint needs more than typical), unlike the current CPU-unit cutoff.

All rounds of success fell in a narrow band (16-18) despite the endpoints' otherwise very
different difficulty for the pooled model -- worth more data before treating that narrowness as a
general property rather than a coincidence of this specific population (4 endpoints, all near-cap
mass, all from the same k7 census tier sample).

=====================================================================================
3. OUTER-SEGMENT ORDERING: DEFICIT VS. BLIND, INCONCLUSIVE AT n=1
=====================================================================================
U000368 was tested with both deficit-ordered and blind (natural enumeration) outer segments.
Both succeeded at exactly round 16; blind actually used FEWER real oracle calls (1,032 vs 1,539).
Not read as "blind order is better" -- at n=1 this is well within noise from which specific new
combinations a round happens to enumerate first before hitting the actual winner. Worth checking
on more endpoints before drawing a conclusion either way; the design's own literature-grounded
expectation (a per-segment order shaping WHICH combinations round r contains) predicts deficit
order should help on average, even if this one comparison didn't show it.

=====================================================================================
4. SELF-LIMITING SEGMENT SATURATION WORKED AS DESIGNED
=====================================================================================
The "cap a segment's radius at its own list size" refinement (discussed before building anything)
showed up correctly in practice: U000535's smallest segment (44 options) saturated at round 17,
after which only the other two segments' radii kept growing (R=[44,51,51] then [44,60,62]).
U000068's two size-12 segments saturated as early as round 11 (R=[13,13,12]), leaving only 2
segments still expanding for the rest of the search. This is exactly the intended effect -- fewer
effectively-growing dimensions as segments exhaust, which should accelerate coverage of what
remains -- and required no special-case code, just the plain min(computed_radius, segment_size)
cap already in the implementation.

=====================================================================================
5. WHAT THIS DOES AND DOES NOT ESTABLISH
=====================================================================================
* n=4 real endpoints, single level of the AND-OR tree, all from the same near-cap-mass k7-census
  population this whole track has focused on. All 4 real, oracle-verified successes -- one matches
  a known census literal winner exactly, the other 3 do not match either known winner for their
  endpoint, confirming (again) these states have more valid solving splits than the census
  recorded.
* Costed in real oracle .ask() calls (each ~50-100ms over the SSM tunnel in this test harness) --
  the wall-clock numbers here (99-267s) reflect Python-plus-network round-trip overhead, NOT what
  an in-process C implementation would cost per equivalent check (which would be orders of
  magnitude faster per call). The call COUNT (1,032-4,399) is the number that would carry over to
  a native implementation's relative cost, not the wall-clock seconds.
* NOT tested: propagating the round/radius down into the recursive verification of the three
  children themselves (the "propagate effort to the level below" half of the original design).
  This test only checked whether ONE state's own segments could be searched this way; the deeper
  claim (this composes well recursively, and absolute-radius propagation gives children
  appropriately more relative effort) is argued from first principles in conversation but not yet
  simulated. That is the natural next step if this single-level result is judged worth pursuing
  further.
* Only one growth target (G=2, doubling total work per round) and one starting radius (R0=1) were
  tried. Not swept.
